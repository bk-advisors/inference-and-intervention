# Speaker Notes — Course Introduction Video

## Opening Hook (30 seconds)

Here is a puzzle that should bother you.

Ethiopia has one of the largest community health worker programs in Africa — over 40,000 Health Extension Workers deployed across the country. But when you look at the data, the regions with the most health workers have the *highest* mortality rates.

Does deploying health workers cause deaths? Obviously not. But if you just looked at the numbers without thinking about causation, you might draw exactly that conclusion — and make a terrible decision as a result.

This course exists so that does not happen to you.

*(pause)*

## What This Course Is About (1 minute)

Welcome to Inference and Intervention.

This is a course about one thing: how to think about cause and effect in complex systems — specifically, health systems where real lives are at stake.

You have probably learned how to look at data — charts, averages, trends. What you probably have not learned is how to think *causally*. Not just "what happened?" but "why did it happen?" and, critically, "what would happen if we did something different?"

Those sound like the same question. They are not. And the gap between them is where this entire course lives.

*(pause)*

## The Journey in Four Parts (3 minutes)

*(Reference the whiteboard diagram here — draw the four-phase pathway)*

Let me walk you through the next ten sessions. Think of it as a journey in four parts.

### Part One: Foundations — Chapters 1 through 3

We start by learning to think causally.

In Chapter 1, I will show you three traps that catch even the smartest analysts — confounding, reverse causation, and selection bias. We will use real examples from Ethiopia's health system.

In Chapter 2, you will learn the three fundamental patterns of causation — chains, forks, and colliders — using Rwanda's community health worker program. Once you understand these three patterns, you understand the logic of every causal model.

In Chapter 3, we build a real causal model from scratch by interviewing people in Kenya's health system — county directors, midwives, community health workers. Each conversation reveals what the previous model missed.

*(pause)*

### Part Two: Adding Numbers — Chapters 4 through 6

Now we put numbers on the picture.

Chapter 4 uses Tanzania's health data to show how to turn your diagram into a calculator. No scary formulas — we count things out of 100 and use simple lookup tables.

Chapter 5 is where the model comes alive. You learn to run it backwards — like a detective working from the crime scene. And you will discover "explaining away" — one of the most surprising ideas in reasoning.

Chapter 6 will surprise you. I will show you how combined data from four countries can tell you the exact *opposite* of what each country's data shows separately. Same numbers, opposite conclusions. Only the causal model can tell you which answer is right.

*(pause)*

### Part Three: Making Decisions — Chapters 7 through 9

Now you are no longer just analyzing — you are deciding.

Chapter 7 introduces the most important distinction: the difference between *watching* and *doing*. Seeing that hospitals with CPAP machines have better outcomes is not the same as giving a hospital a CPAP machine. We use Kenya's counties to work through real decisions.

Chapter 8 scales up. You have a limited budget and four countries — Ethiopia, Rwanda, Kenya, Tanzania. How do you split it? We explore three strategies and simulate thousands of possible futures.

Chapter 9 adds the hardest layer. Other people react to your decisions. When you invest in a country, the government might pull back its own spending. You will learn how Rwanda's Mutuelle de Santé prevents this free-rider problem.

*(pause)*

### Part Four: Learning from Data — Chapter 10

Finally, we close the loop. Can computers figure out the causal arrows from data alone? Partially — but they need human experts to get it right. We use Tanzania's DHIS2 health data system to show how this works.

*(pause — draw the feedback arrow on the whiteboard)*

And notice the arrow going back from the end to the beginning. Data refines the model. The model guides new data collection. You iterate. That is how understanding grows.

## Who This Course Is For (30 seconds)

This course is for anyone who wants to make better decisions about health — or about anything, really. You do not need to be a math expert. You do not need to know programming, though we will use a bit of R. You just need to be curious about how the world works and willing to draw your ideas on a whiteboard.

## What You Will Walk Away With (30 seconds)

By the end of these ten sessions, you will have:

- A way of thinking about cause and effect that goes beyond just looking at data
- The ability to draw, quantify, and question causal models
- Tools for making decisions when you are not sure what will happen
- A healthy suspicion of any number that does not come with a causal story attached

## Closing (15 seconds)

The gap between good intentions and good outcomes is not a gap of resources. It is a gap of causal reasoning. This course is designed to close that gap.

I will see you in Chapter 1.
