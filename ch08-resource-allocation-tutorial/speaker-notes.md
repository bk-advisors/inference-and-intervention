# Chapter 8 Speaker Notes — Spreading the Money

**Commander's Intent:** Spreading resources is not a math question — it is a question about which uncertainties you are willing to live with.

**Plot:** Connection — a programme officer balancing a portfolio across four countries with different risk shapes.

**Protagonist:** Grace, 40, programme officer at a global health partnership. Composite from public donor reports.

**Estimated runtime:** 22–26 minutes.

## Overview (before slide 1)

Welcome back. *(pause)*

Last chapter, David in Nakuru had one envelope and two facilities. One decision. Two options. Today we make it harder. *(pause)*

Today, we have a fixed budget and *four countries*. Not "where does the midwife go" — but *"how do I split a portfolio across many places at once, knowing that some will deliver and some will disappoint, and I don't know in advance which is which?"* *(pause)*

This is the chapter most directly useful for grant managers, programme officers, and donors. If you ever sit on a committee that allocates money across geographies, this is your chapter. *(pause)*

We are going to meet someone named Grace. She works at a global health partnership. And her board meets in three weeks.

---

## Slide: Meet Grace

Grace is forty years old. She is a programme officer at a global health partnership — one of those organisations that pools money from multiple donors and redistributes it to country MNH programmes. *(pause)*

Same disclosure. Grace is a composite. She is drawn from publicly available donor reports — the role and constraints are real, the name is not. *(pause)*

She has just received approval for a new four-country MNH portfolio. Her budget is limited. We will not put a number on it because the principles work the same at any size. The four countries are the four countries we have been using all along: **Ethiopia, Rwanda, Kenya, and Tanzania.** *(pause)*

Her board meets in three weeks. *(pause)* And the board wants one slide. *(pause)* *"How are you splitting the money?"* *(pause)*

That is the question. One slide. Four countries. A board waiting in three weeks. *(pause)*

Look at the box on the screen. **Concentrate it on the country most likely to deliver? Spread it evenly? Tier it? Each strategy has a different shape of risk — and Grace needs to see those shapes before she walks into the room.** *(pause)*

That is what this chapter teaches. The shapes of risk.

---

## Slide: And here is the puzzle

Grace starts the way most people start. She runs the numbers under the simplest assumption — equal split. Each country gets one quarter of the budget. *(pause)*

She uses public sources — WHO, DHS, country MOH reports — to get a rough estimate of expected lives saved per quarter-budget for each country. *(pause)*

*Look at the table.* Ethiopia: twelve hundred lives, with a range from six hundred to eighteen hundred. Rwanda: nine hundred, with a tighter range — seven hundred to eleven hundred. Kenya: a thousand, range four hundred to sixteen hundred. Tanzania: eleven hundred, range five hundred to seventeen hundred. *(pause)*

Total expected: forty-two hundred lives saved. *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **The expected total looks fine. The range is enormous.** Twenty-two hundred to sixty-two hundred. *(pause)*

Read those two numbers. Twenty-two hundred. Sixty-two hundred. *(pause)* That is the difference between *"we did okay"* and *"we missed by half"*. The same portfolio, the same money, the same plan — and the actual outcome might be either of those, depending on dozens of things Grace cannot fully control. *(pause)*

This is why expected value alone is not enough. The number forty-two hundred hides everything that matters about the *risk* of the portfolio. To make a real decision, Grace needs to know the *shape* of the range, not just the centre of it.

---

## Slide: Three strategies, three risk shapes (section divider)

*(pause for transition)*

There are three classic strategies for spreading a budget across uncertain options. Concentrate. Spread. Tier. We are going to look at each one in turn — and pay attention to the *risk shape*, not just the average.

---

## Slide: Strategy 1 — Concentrate

Strategy one. **Concentrate.** *(pause)*

Take the country with the highest expected return — that's Ethiopia at twelve hundred lives saved per quarter-budget — and put *all* the money there. *(pause)*

The argument is simple: pick the winner, double down. *(pause)*

If you scale up four-fold — taking a quarter-budget effect of twelve hundred and applying four times the resources — *maybe* you save four times as much. Forty-eight hundred lives. *(pause)*

But scale-ups run into bottlenecks. The same Ethiopia that can absorb a quarter-budget might not absorb a full budget without running into capacity limits, training shortfalls, supply-chain congestion. So the actual range under concentration is wider — maybe fifteen hundred to seven thousand. *(pause)*

*Bullet by bullet.* Best case is huge. Seven thousand. *(pause)* Worst case is the worst of any strategy — fifteen hundred. That is the lowest bottom in any of the three strategies. *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **Concentration is a bet.** It is the right move if you have very strong evidence about the chosen country, *and* a high tolerance for downside. *(pause)*

The problem is that very few boards have a high tolerance for downside. Boards remember the bad years for ten years. They forget the good years in three.

---

## Slide: Strategy 2 — Spread evenly

Strategy two. **Spread evenly.** *(pause)*

A quarter to each country. The original forty-two hundred number, with the original range — twenty-two hundred to sixty-two hundred. *(pause)*

*Bullet by bullet.* No country can crash the whole portfolio. If Tanzania has a terrible year, the other three are still doing their work — the loss is bounded. *(pause)* But also, no country can singlehandedly carry it. Even if Rwanda has a spectacular year, the upside is limited because most of the money is sitting elsewhere. *(pause)*

Look at the box. **The safe strategy.** Lower upside than concentration, much smaller downside. *(pause)*

This is the strategy that gets approved at most boards. It is also the strategy that is rarely celebrated. Spreading evenly is what you do when you do not want anyone to lose. It is also what you do when you do not want anyone to *win* especially big.

---

## Slide: Strategy 3 — Tier

Strategy three. **Tier.** *(pause)*

This one is the most interesting. Two countries get forty percent each — the two countries with the strongest evidence behind them. The other two get ten percent each — kept as smaller learning bets, so that if they prove out, you can scale them up next year. *(pause)*

*Bullet by bullet.* Most of the money rides on the strongest evidence. Eighty percent of the budget is going to the two best-supported options. *(pause)* The smaller bets keep two countries warm. Ten percent is enough to maintain a presence, run a small programme, learn whether the country is ready for more. *(pause)* And the range — twenty-eight hundred to sixty-five hundred — is *narrower than concentration on the upside* but with a much higher floor. *(pause)*

Look at the green box. **The portfolio strategy.** Higher floor than concentrate, higher ceiling than spread. *(pause)*

Most experienced grant officers gravitate to this one. Once you have done a few funding cycles, you stop loving concentration (because the bad year ruins your career) and you stop loving even-spread (because nothing ever moves the needle), and you start looking for the tier.

---

## Slide: A side-by-side picture

Here is the comparison.

*Look at the table.* Concentrate: expected forty-eight hundred, worst case fifteen hundred, best case seven thousand. Spread: expected forty-two hundred, worst twenty-two hundred, best sixty-two hundred. Tier: expected forty-five hundred, worst twenty-eight hundred, best sixty-five hundred. *(pause)*

Now look at this carefully. *(pause)*

Concentrate has the highest expected value. That is true. But it also has the *worst* worst case. *(pause)*

Spread has the lowest expected value. But it has the smallest range — the most predictable outcome. *(pause)*

Tier sits in the middle on expected value. But — and this matters — its *floor is higher* than spread's floor. Twenty-eight hundred versus twenty-two hundred. And its ceiling is also higher. *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **The expected value alone does not pick a winner.** The shape of the range — the distance between worst and best, where the bulk of the futures sit — does much of the work. *(pause)*

If Grace walks into her board with only the expected values, the board will pick concentrate and feel clever. If she walks in with the *full picture*, including the worst cases and the best cases, she will get a much better conversation — and probably a better decision.

## Slide: Seeing the shape with simulation (section divider)

*(pause for transition)*

OK. So how does Grace actually *show* the shape of the range to her board? The answer is the most powerful tool in the chapter. It is also surprisingly easy to explain.

---

## Slide: Why one number is not enough

A single expected value tells you the *average* over all the futures. It does not tell you what *most* of the futures look like. *(pause)*

Look at the box. **For Grace's board, the question is not "what is the average?". The question is "could we end up under three thousand lives saved? How likely is that?"** *(pause)*

That question — *"how likely is the worst case?"* — is the question every board chair is silently asking. And an average cannot answer it. You need to *see the distribution*.

---

## Slide: Monte Carlo, in plain language

There is a tool for this. It has a flashy name — **Monte Carlo simulation** — and people get scared by the name. They should not. The idea is simple. *(pause)*

Look at the definition box. **Instead of asking "what is the average outcome?", you play out many possible futures — say, ten thousand futures — using the picture and the lookup tables. Then you count how many of those futures landed where.** *(pause)*

That is it. You take your picture from Chapter 2. You take your lookup tables from Chapter 4. You add a tiny bit of randomness — pull a number from each lookup table. You compute the outcome for that one future. You write it down. Then you do it ten thousand more times. *(pause)*

The output is not a single number. The output is a *distribution* — a histogram showing the *shape* of risk. *(pause)*

Look at the green box. **You can read it like a weather forecast.** *"There is a twenty percent chance of less than three thousand lives saved."* That is a sentence a board member can understand. That is a sentence a board member can actually argue with. *(pause)*

We will do this for real on the companion page using R. But the *idea* is what matters. Many futures, played out, counted up.

---

## Slide: What Grace's three histograms look like

When Grace runs the simulation for the three strategies, she gets three different histograms. They look very different. *(pause)*

*Bullet by bullet.* Concentrate gives her a tall narrow bell shifted to the right — most of the futures are great, the average is high — but there is a *long left tail*. A small fraction of futures are catastrophic. The shape is *"usually amazing, occasionally terrible"*. *(pause)*

Spread gives her a fat blob in the middle. Almost no futures are great. Almost no futures are terrible. Most are *okay*. The shape is *"reliable"*. *(pause)*

Tier gives her something in between — a bell shifted slightly left of concentrate, with a much shorter left tail. It catches most of the upside, but cuts off most of the downside. *(pause)*

Look at the box. **That difference in shape is the conversation Grace needs to have with her board.** Not a fight about whose expected value is bigger. A fight about *what kind of failure mode the partnership can live with*. *(pause)*

That is the right conversation. And almost no boards have it, because almost no analysts show them the histograms.

## Slide: Three quiet warnings (section divider)

*(pause for transition)*

---

## Slide: Warning 1 — Probabilities of countries are not independent

Warning one. **The countries are not independent.** *(pause)*

If a regional drought hits East Africa, it affects Ethiopia, Kenya, *and* Tanzania at the same time. If a new infectious-disease outbreak crosses borders, it does not respect grant boundaries. If global supply chains collapse, every country's stockout problem gets worse simultaneously. *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **Spreading across countries that share the same risk is not real diversification.** It just feels like it. *(pause)*

The fix is to think about *which* risks are correlated — and try to spread across uncorrelated ones, even if they are not the most attractive on their own. Real diversification spreads across *risks*, not just across *places*.

---

## Slide: Warning 2 — The "tier" strategy assumes you will follow up

Warning two. **The tier strategy assumes you will use the learning.** *(pause)*

The whole point of putting ten percent into a small bet is that you will *learn* from it. Did the country show progress? Did the conditions change? Should we scale it up next year? *(pause)*

If next year's grant cycle ignores the small bet's results — because the budget process is locked in, or the staff turned over, or nobody read the report — then you have wasted the ten percent. *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **Tiering only works if there is a feedback loop.** Build the review meeting into the grant agreement, so that the small bets are evaluated on a fixed schedule and the results actually feed into the next allocation. Without that, tiering is just a cosmetic version of concentration.

---

## Slide: Warning 3 — The numbers came from somewhere

Warning three is the same warning we have given in every Part 3 chapter, because it is the most important one. *(pause)*

Every cell in Grace's table started as someone's *guess*. The guesses were informed — by DHS surveys, by country reports, by interviews with country teams — but they were guesses. *(pause)*

Look at the red box. **A beautiful Monte Carlo on top of bad inputs is dangerous.** *(pause)*

The simulation gives you the shape of the risk you can *see*. It cannot show you the risks you forgot to put in. If your model assumes Ethiopia and Tanzania have independent outcomes, and the simulation gives you a beautifully narrow distribution, the narrowness is *fake* — it comes from a missing arrow on the picture. *(pause)*

Always go back to Chapter 2. Always check whether there is a parent box that links your supposedly-independent things.

---

## Slide: Try It (You are the analyst)

OK. Try it.

You have a fixed MNH budget. Three options. *(pause)* Option A: a national rollout in a country with strong recent results but moderate uncertainty. Option B: a regional pilot in a country with weak recent results but very high upside if it works. Option C: a fifty-fifty split between A and B. *(pause)*

**Sketch the three risk shapes — concentrate-on-A, concentrate-on-B, fifty-fifty — without computing anything. Which one would you bring to the board, and why?** *(pause)*

A hint. A strategy with a high average and a long left tail is *not* the same risk profile as a strategy with a slightly lower average and *no* left tail. Boards often forget this. Your job is to remind them. *(pause)*

If you sketched concentrate-on-A as a tall narrow bell, concentrate-on-B as a fat ugly distribution with a heavy left tail and a long right tail, and fifty-fifty as something in between — you have the right idea. The right answer depends on the board's appetite for variance. There is no single correct call. The skill is in *making the trade-off visible*.

## Slide: Looking ahead

So that is Chapter 8.

In Chapter 9 we add a wrinkle that changes everything. *(pause)*

So far in Part 3, we have been treating the world as something Grace acts on. She decides, the world responds, and the outcome happens. *(pause)*

But the world has *people in it*. And people *react to your decisions*. A donor's grant rarely lands in a vacuum. Governments redirect their own money in response. Other donors fill or do not fill the gaps. Some counties take the deal eagerly. Others quietly redirect to other priorities. *(pause)*

The tool for thinking about that is **game theory**. And it is going to teach us why the simple expected-value calculation Grace just did can be very wrong if she does not account for how other people will respond to her choices. *(pause)*
