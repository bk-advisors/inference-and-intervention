The big idea: When resources are scarce — and they always are — you need to know what causes what before you can decide what to do. This chapter introduces the thinking tools that make that possible.

Situational Assessment

What is happening? What are the causes?

Here’s the Puzzle

Ethiopia has one of the largest community health worker programs in Africa — over 40,000 Health Extension Workers deployed across the country.

But here is something strange:

The regions where the most Health Extension Workers are deployed tend to have the highest mortality rates.

Does deploying health workers cause deaths?

Obviously not. But if you just looked at the numbers — workers deployed vs. mortality rates — that is exactly what the data would seem to say.

This is the kind of puzzle that causal thinking solves. The data is not lying. But it is not telling the whole story either. By the end of this chapter, you will be able to explain exactly what is going on.

The Manager’s Two Questions

Every decision-maker faces two fundamental questions:

Question 1: What is happening?

Situational Assessment — understanding the current state by gathering evidence and reasoning about causes.

“Why are so many mothers still dying in Amhara region despite having more health workers than anywhere else?”

Question 2: What should I do?

Managerial Intervention — choosing which levers to pull to change outcomes.

“Should we train more midwives, buy more equipment, or fix the data system?”

Key insight: You cannot answer Question 2 well until you have answered Question 1 well. Acting without understanding causes is like prescribing medicine without a diagnosis.

What Is Situational Assessment?

Situational Assessment: Using evidence to reason about the current state of a system — figuring out which variables are in which states, and why.

Think of it this way:

  • A doctor examining a patient: What symptoms are present? What do they point to?
  • A mechanic diagnosing a car: The engine is overheating — but is it the coolant, the fan, or a cracked gasket?
  • A health program manager in Ethiopia: Neonatal mortality is not falling in Somali region — is it a workforce problem, a supply chain problem, or something else entirely?

In each case, the person is trying to figure out what is causing what before deciding how to act. Causal models give us a systematic way to do this.

Here is the situation Ethiopia faces, based on publicly available data:

Managerial Intervention

What should I do? Which levers should I pull?

Managers Manage Scarce Resources

Managerial Intervention: A decision about which things to control or change — the choice of what to act on, before deciding how to act.

  • Resources are always limited — money, time, people, equipment
  • Every investment involves trade-offs: spending on training means not spending on equipment
  • Choosing wisely requires understanding what causes what

Imagine you are running a health program in Ethiopia with a fixed budget. You have to choose how to divide your resources. Here are three options:

The Trade-Off

Option A: People

Train 5,000 more midwives in emergency obstetric care. Staff every health center with a skilled birth attendant.

Theory: More trained staff → better care → fewer deaths.

Option B: Products

Buy CPAP machines for newborn breathing support, PPH kits for hemorrhage treatment, and ultrasound devices for every district hospital.

Theory: Better equipment → better treatment → fewer deaths.

Option C: Systems

Build a real-time data dashboard (DHIS2), a referral transport network, and a supply chain tracking system.

Theory: Better information → better decisions → better care → fewer deaths.

Here is the problem: All three sound reasonable. But you cannot do all three equally well. So which one will cause the greatest reduction in mortality? That is a causal question — and you cannot answer it with a spreadsheet full of correlations.

Why You Need Causal Thinking to Decide

Consider what happens if you skip the causal reasoning and just look at the data:

What the data might show

Regions with the most equipment have the lowest mortality.

Naive conclusion: Buy more equipment!

What is actually going on

Regions with the most equipment are also the wealthiest, most urban regions — they would have had lower mortality anyway.

The equipment is not causing the low mortality. Wealth is causing both.

Without causal thinking, data can lead you to exactly the wrong decision.

The rest of this chapter teaches you why this happens and how to avoid it.

Correlation ≠ Causation

The three traps of causal reasoning

The Ice Cream Problem

Before we go back to health data, here is a classic example everyone can relate to:

Everyday Analogy

Observation: On days when ice cream sales are high, drowning deaths are also high.

Naive conclusion: Ice cream causes drowning!

Reality: Hot weather causes both. People eat more ice cream in summer. People also swim more in summer. Summer heat is the hidden third variable connecting both.

This hidden-third-variable problem has a name: confounding. And it is just one of three traps that make people confuse correlation with causation.

Trap 1: Confounding

Confounding occurs when a third variable influences both the supposed cause and the supposed effect, creating a misleading association between them.

MNH Example

Observation: Regions in Ethiopia with more Health Extension Workers deployed have higher mortality rates.

Naive conclusion: Health workers cause deaths?

Reality: More health workers are deployed to the sickest regions — places with the highest disease burden. Disease burden is the confounder. It drives both health worker deployment (because that is where the need is) and mortality (because that is where disease is worst).

Health Worker
Deployment
←
Disease
Burden
→
Neonatal
Mortality

Read the diagram: Disease burden sends arrows to both health worker deployment and mortality. That shared cause creates a correlation between workers and mortality — even though workers actually help.

Trap 2: Reverse Causation

Reverse Causation occurs when the supposed effect actually causes the supposed cause — the arrow points the wrong way.

MNH Example

Observation: Countries that receive more international health funding tend to have higher mortality rates.

Naive conclusion: Foreign aid increases mortality?

Reality: High mortality attracts funding. Donors send money to the countries with the worst health outcomes. The causal arrow runs from mortality to funding, not the other way around.

What people assume:

More Funding → Higher Mortality

What is actually happening:

Higher Mortality → More Funding

Think of it this way: Seeing more firefighters at bigger fires does not mean firefighters cause fires. They are responding to them. Same logic.

Trap 3: Selection Bias

Selection Bias occurs when the data sample is not representative of the population, because which cases end up in your data depends on the very things you are trying to study.

MNH Example

Observation: Among hospitals that report data to Ethiopia’s DHIS2 system, CPAP machine availability appears unrelated to neonatal survival.

Problem: Only well-resourced hospitals consistently report data to DHIS2. These hospitals already have better outcomes. The hospitals where CPAP machines would make the biggest difference — small, under-resourced facilities — are missing from the data entirely.

Selection mechanism: Hospital capacity determines both whether the hospital reports data and how well its babies survive.

The danger: If you only study the hospitals you can see in the data, you might conclude that equipment does not matter — when in fact it matters enormously for the facilities you cannot see.

Three Traps: Summary

Trap What Goes Wrong Ethiopia Example
Confounding A hidden third variable drives both X and Y Disease burden drives both worker deployment and mortality
Reverse Causation Y causes X, not X causes Y Mortality attracts international funding
Selection Bias Your data sample is not representative Only well-resourced hospitals report to DHIS2

All three traps create patterns in the data that look like causal effects but are not.

The antidote? Explicit causal modeling — drawing a picture of what causes what before you analyze the data.

The Causal Model Framework

Nodes, arrows, and directed graphs

What Is a Causal Model?

Think of it this way: a causal model is just a diagram that shows what causes what.

It has three simple ingredients:

  • Nodes — the things you care about (drawn as circles, rectangles, or other shapes)
  • Arrows — causal links from one thing to another (“A causes B” is drawn as A → B)
  • Signs — [+] or [-] telling you the direction of effect (does more of A mean more of B, or less?)

The key rule: An arrow from A to B means that changing A can change B. But changing B does not change A. Arrows have a direction — and the direction matters.

What Is a DAG?

Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG): A diagram where all arrows point in one direction and there are no loops — you can never follow the arrows from any node back to itself. “Directed” means arrows have a direction. “Acyclic” means no circles. “Graph” is just the math word for a diagram of connected nodes.

Valid DAG (no loops)

A → B → C

You can follow the arrows forward but never get back to where you started.

Not a valid DAG (has a loop)

A → B → C → A

Following the arrows brings you back to A. This is a cycle, not a DAG.

Why does this matter? Loops make it impossible to figure out what causes what — everything causes everything. DAGs force you to be clear and specific about the direction of causation.

A Simple 3-Node Chain

Here is the simplest useful causal model for a health program:

Investment
→ [+]
Intervention
Coverage
→ [-]
Maternal
Mortality
  • Investment → Coverage [+]: More funding increases the percentage of mothers who receive skilled care
  • Coverage → Mortality [-]: Higher coverage of life-saving interventions reduces maternal deaths
  • This is a chain — investment does not directly reduce mortality; it works through coverage

This tiny model already tells us something useful: if investment goes up but coverage does not go up, the money is being wasted somewhere in between. The chain helps you find where things break down.

But Wait — It Is Not That Simple

The 3-node chain is a good start, but it ignores a lot. What about:

  • Confounders like disease burden, that affect both where investment goes and how high mortality is?
  • Multiple pathways — investment might improve training, equipment, and data systems, each affecting mortality differently?
  • Quality of care as a mediator between inputs and outcomes?

That is what the rest of the course is about. In Chapter 2, we will learn the formal rules for building these diagrams. In Chapters 4-6, we will add numbers. In Chapters 7-8, we will use them to make decisions. For now, just understand the basic building blocks: nodes, arrows, and direction.

MNH Application: Ethiopia

Causal thinking applied to real health decisions

The causal question: How do these three pillars combine to produce better outcomes? Do they work independently, or do they need each other?

Think of it this way: a trained midwife (People) without a PPH kit (Products) cannot stop a hemorrhage. A PPH kit without a trained midwife is just a box on a shelf. And neither one helps if the mother cannot get to the facility (Systems).

People, Products, and Systems are complements — they multiply each other’s effect.

Why the Numbers Mislead

Now let’s go back to our opening puzzle. If you looked at raw data across Ethiopia’s regions:

What the data shows

Regions with the most Health Extension Workers per capita also have the highest maternal mortality.

Regions with the most equipment also tend to have high mortality.

A naive analyst might say: “These investments are not working!”

What the causal model reveals

The regions with the highest disease burden — think Somali, Afar, Gambella — receive the most resources because they have the worst outcomes.

Confounding by disease burden creates a positive correlation between resources and mortality, even when resources are saving lives.

This is exactly Trap 1 (confounding) in action. The solution is not to ignore the data. The solution is to build a causal model that includes disease burden as a confounder, and then analyze the data through that model.

ANC Coverage: A Closer Look

Ethiopia’s antenatal care (ANC) coverage shows how much progress has been made — and how far there is to go:

  • ~74% of pregnant women attend at least one ANC visit (DHS 2019)
  • But only about 43% complete the recommended four or more visits
  • Urban coverage is much higher than rural coverage — another confounder
  • The leading causes of maternal death — hemorrhage (~25%), hypertension (~16%), sepsis (~11%) — are all treatable if mothers reach skilled care in time

Here is the causal chain: ANC visits → Early detection of complications → Timely referral → Skilled treatment → Survival. Every link in this chain must work. If any one link breaks — a missed visit, a failed referral, an absent midwife, a missing drug — the outcome can be death.

This is why causal models matter for real decisions. A naive analysis might say “ANC does not reduce mortality” if coverage goes up but deaths stay constant. A causal model asks: where in the chain is it breaking?

R Workshop: Your First Causal Diagrams

Using dagitty and ggdag in R

Setting Up the R Environment

Before we draw any diagrams, we need to load our tools. The code below loads three R packages that we will use throughout this course.

# Install these packages the first time you use them (remove the # to run):
# install.packages(c("dagitty", "ggdag", "ggplot2"))

# Load the packages into our R session
library(dagitty)    # dagitty lets us define and analyze causal diagrams (DAGs)
library(ggdag)      # ggdag draws pretty pictures of those DAGs
library(ggplot2)    # ggplot2 is the general-purpose plotting library

What do these packages do?

  • dagitty — lets you write down a causal diagram in code and ask questions about it (like “what do I need to control for?”)
  • ggdag — turns that code into a visual diagram you can look at
  • ggplot2 — the engine that powers the drawings (ggdag uses it behind the scenes)

R: Drawing Our Simple 3-Node Chain

Let’s start by drawing the simple Investment → Coverage → Mortality chain we saw earlier. The code below defines three nodes and two arrows, then draws the picture.

# Step 1: Define the DAG
# We list nodes with positions (pos="x,y") and arrows (->)
mnh_simple <- dagitty('dag {
  Investment [pos="0,0"]
  Coverage   [pos="1,0"]
  Mortality  [pos="2,0"]

  Investment -> Coverage
  Coverage -> Mortality
}')

# Step 2: Draw it
# ggdag() takes our DAG definition and produces a plot
# theme_dag() removes the gridlines and axes to keep it clean
ggdag(mnh_simple) +
  theme_dag() +
  labs(title = "Simple MNH Causal Model",
       subtitle = "Investment → Coverage → Mortality")

What to notice: The arrows only go left-to-right. Investment affects Coverage, and Coverage affects Mortality. There is no arrow from Investment directly to Mortality — the effect goes through Coverage.

R: Adding a Confounder

Now let’s add Disease Burden as a confounder — the hidden variable that was creating the misleading correlation between health workers and mortality. This is the confounding diagram from our Ethiopia example.

# Define the confounded model
# Disease_Burden affects BOTH Health_Workers and Mortality
confounded_model <- dagitty('dag {
  Disease_Burden [pos="1,0"]
  Health_Workers [pos="0,1"]
  Mortality      [pos="2,1"]

  Disease_Burden -> Health_Workers
  Disease_Burden -> Mortality
  Health_Workers -> Mortality
}')

# Draw it with color-coding:
# - "exposure" = the thing we think is the cause (Health_Workers)
# - "outcome" = the thing we are measuring (Mortality)
ggdag_status(confounded_model,
             exposure = "Health_Workers",
             outcome = "Mortality") +
  theme_dag() +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("exposure" = "#83BD00",    # green for treatment
                                "outcome" = "#E24A3F",     # red for outcome
                                "other" = "#F8A623")) +    # amber for confounder
  labs(title = "Confounding: Health Workers and Mortality",
       subtitle = "Disease burden drives both — creating a misleading correlation")

Read the picture: Disease Burden sits at the top, sending arrows down to both Health Workers and Mortality. That is the fork structure — the classic confounding pattern. Without accounting for Disease Burden, you would wrongly conclude that health workers increase mortality.

R: What Do We Need to Control For?

Here is the powerful part. Once we have drawn the diagram, R can tell us exactly which variables we need to control for to get the true causal effect.

# Ask dagitty: "What do I need to control for to estimate the
# causal effect of Health_Workers on Mortality?"
adjustmentSets(confounded_model,
               exposure = "Health_Workers",
               outcome = "Mortality")

# This should return: { Disease_Burden }
# Translation: "Control for Disease Burden, and the confounding goes away."

What this means in plain English: If you compare regions that have the same disease burden, then the regions with more health workers will have lower mortality. The confounding disappears once you account for why workers were deployed where they were.

This is the whole point of the course in one slide. Draw the causal diagram. Ask R what to control for. Analyze the data correctly. Make better decisions. We will build on this foundation in every chapter that follows.

R: Ethiopia’s Three-Pillar Model

Let’s build the richer model that includes Ethiopia’s three-pillar approach — People, Products, and Systems — with Disease Burden as a confounder.

# Define Ethiopia's three-pillar theory of change as a DAG
ethiopia_toc <- dagitty('dag {
  People          [pos="1,0"]
  Products        [pos="1,1"]
  Systems         [pos="1,2"]
  Quality         [pos="2,1"]
  NMR             [pos="3,0.5"]
  MMR             [pos="3,1.5"]
  Disease_Burden  [pos="2,2.5"]

  People   -> Quality
  Products -> Quality
  Systems  -> Quality
  Quality  -> NMR
  Quality  -> MMR
  Disease_Burden -> NMR
  Disease_Burden -> MMR
}')

# Draw the full model
ggdag(ethiopia_toc) +
  theme_dag() +
  labs(title = "Ethiopia MNH: Three-Pillar Theory of Change",
       subtitle = "People, Products, and Systems → Quality → Mortality")

What this model says: All three pillars flow into Quality of Care. Quality then reduces both NMR and MMR. Disease Burden affects the outcomes directly — a region with severe malaria burden will have higher mortality even with good care.

R: Finding All the Paths

We can also ask R to show us every causal pathway from a starting point to an endpoint. This helps us see all the different routes through which an investment could affect mortality.

# What are all the paths from People (workforce investment) to NMR?
people_to_nmr <- paths(ethiopia_toc,
                        from = "People",
                        to = "NMR")

# Print the paths — each one is a different causal route
print(people_to_nmr)

# What variables must we control for to isolate
# the effect of People on NMR?
adjustmentSets(ethiopia_toc,
               exposure = "People",
               outcome = "NMR")

Key Takeaways

Causal thinking starts with two questions: “What is happening?” (situational assessment) and “What should I do?” (managerial intervention). You cannot answer the second well without answering the first.

Correlation does not equal causation. Three traps — confounding, reverse causation, and selection bias — all create misleading patterns in data.

A causal model (DAG) makes your assumptions visible, testable, and actionable. It tells you what to control for, where the chain breaks, and which interventions will actually work.

Ethiopia’s 40,000 Health Extension Workers are not causing deaths. Disease burden is confounding the relationship. Causal models help us see through the numbers to the truth.
