What Is This Course?

A practical guide on how to think about cause and effect in complex health systems.

You will deal with messy data, competing explanations, and hard trade-offs.

To make good decisions, you need to tell apart what is really causing an outcome from what just happens to show up alongside it.

Causal thinking will help you do that.

In this course, I will teach you the essential tools of causal inference that you will need when designing, managing, or evaluating health programs.

Who Is This Course For?

This course is mainly for anyone who makes decisions about health programs — or wants to.

  • Program managers and coordinators
  • Policy advisors and planners
  • Grants and portfolio managers
  • M&E and data analysts
  • Students interested in global health

You don’t need a math degree or programming background. I’ll break everything down simply.

Three Things You Will Learn

  1. How to draw your thinking. You will learn to build causal diagrams — simple pictures that show what causes what — before touching any data.

  2. How to avoid common traps. You will learn to spot confounding, reverse causation, selection bias, and Simpson’s Paradox — mistakes that mislead even experienced analysts.

  3. How to make better decisions. You will learn to move from “what happened?” to “what should we do?” — using decision theory, resource allocation, and game theory.

How the Course Is Organized

Course Structure

We have divided the course into three parts and ten chapters.


Part Chapters What You Learn
1. Draw the Picture Ch 1–3 Correlation vs. causation, causal diagrams, building models from interviews
2. Add the Numbers Ch 4–6 Probability, updating beliefs with evidence, Simpson’s Paradox
3. Make Decisions Ch 7–10 Observing vs. intervening, resource allocation, game theory, data vs. expert judgment

Part 1: Draw the Picture (Ch 1–3)

We start by learning to tell apart correlation from causation.

Using examples from Ethiopia’s health workforce, you will see how numbers can mislead.

Then you will learn to draw causal diagrams — simple boxes-and-arrows sketches — using Rwanda’s community health program.

Finally, a hands-on case study across Kenya’s 47 counties where you build a causal model through interviews.

Key idea: Sketch your assumptions on paper before you look at any data.

Part 2: Add the Numbers (Ch 4–6)

Once you have the picture, we put numbers on it.

  • Probability — a way of being honest about what you don’t know yet
  • Bayesian thinking — updating your beliefs when new evidence comes in
  • Simpson’s Paradox — when combined data says one thing, but each country’s data says the opposite

Watch out: Aggregated trends across countries can hide — or reverse — what is really happening.

Part 3: Make Decisions (Ch 7–10)

This is where it gets practical.

You will learn the difference between watching something happen and making it happen.

  • Ch 7: Observational vs. experimental evidence — allocating devices like breathing machines for newborns
  • Ch 8: Portfolio thinking — spreading limited resources across countries
  • Ch 9: Game theory — governments react to your investments, sometimes matching your money, sometimes redirecting their own
  • Ch 10: Can data alone discover causes? Partly — but you still need expert judgment

By the end: You will be able to draw causal diagrams, spot analytic traps, and make sharper decisions with scarce resources.
