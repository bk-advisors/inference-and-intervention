Meet Beatrice

Beatrice, 34, Northern Province, Rwanda (a composite drawn from DHS Rwanda 2019/20 narratives — the details are real, the name is not)

Beatrice is an Animatrice de Santé — one of Rwanda’s roughly 45,000 community health workers, almost all of them women, almost all of them volunteers (Rwanda Biomedical Centre, 2020).

She has six days of training, a bicycle, a phone, and a list of every pregnant woman in three nearby villages.

She is not a nurse. She is not a midwife. She is not paid a salary.

The question: How can a volunteer with six days of training help drive one of the fastest declines in newborn mortality on record?

And here is the puzzle

In 2000, Rwanda’s neonatal mortality rate was 41 deaths per 1,000 live births (DHS Rwanda 2000).

By 2019, it had fallen to 19 per 1,000 (DHS Rwanda 2019/20).

Less than half. In one generation. With volunteers like Beatrice doing most of the front-line work.

Something in the system is making volunteers far more powerful than their training would suggest. What?

We need a better picture

Words are not enough

People say things like “community health workers reduce mortality through early referral and behaviour change.”

That sentence has six nouns and zero arrows. You cannot draw it. You cannot test it. You cannot tell what would break if you changed one piece.

This chapter teaches you how to turn that kind of sentence into a picture you can actually use.

Three things a box can mean

In our pictures, every box is one of three things:

  • A factor — something that varies but you cannot directly choose. Beatrice’s eligible villages are mostly poor or mostly not poor.
  • An action — something a person actively decides to do. Beatrice visits a pregnant woman, or she does not.
  • An outcome — the thing you ultimately care about. The newborn survives, or does not.

Same shape on the page, three different roles. Knowing which is which is most of the work.

Beatrice’s three boxes


Mother is poor Beatrice visits Newborn survives


  • The factor is on the left. Beatrice cannot choose how poor a household is.
  • The action is in the middle. Beatrice can choose to visit, or not.
  • The outcome is on the right. That is the thing we are trying to move.

Arrows have signs

An arrow does not just say “this affects that”. It also says which direction.

  • A positive arrow means “more of this leads to more of that”. More antenatal visits → more birth-preparedness.
  • A negative arrow means “more of this leads to less of that”. More distance to clinic → less likely to deliver there.

A picture without signs is half a picture. (We will mark them with + or − next to the arrow.)

Beatrice’s picture, with signs


Mother is poor −→ Beatrice visits +→ Newborn survives



Mother is poor −−−−−−−−−−−−→ Newborn survives

Two arrows point at the outcome. That is the start of the puzzle: which one is doing the work?

Three shapes that show up everywhere

Shape 1 — The chain

Three boxes in a row. A causes B, which causes C.


Beatrice visits → Mother knows danger signs → Mother goes to clinic in time

The middle box passes the message along. Block the middle box (no danger-signs message) and the chain breaks.

Shape 2 — The fork

One box causes two others. The two are not connected to each other — they just share a parent.


Government rolls out CHW programme

⬇ ⬇

Beatrice gets training New ambulance lands in district

This is the trap from Chapter 1. Beatrice and the ambulance look like they cause each other. They do not. They share a parent.

Shape 3 — The collider

Two boxes both cause a third. The third is the collider.


Beatrice visits Distance to clinic is short

⬇ ⬇

Mother delivers in clinic

The strange thing about colliders: the two parents are unrelated until you look at families who delivered in the clinic. Then they suddenly look related — and that “relationship” is fake.

We’ll come back to this in Chapter 6. For now, just remember the shape.

Putting it together

You can see chains, forks, and colliders all at once. This is what a useful picture looks like.

So how do volunteers drive that much change?

Look at the picture. Beatrice’s training is one box. Her visits are one box. But the outcome at the end has three different paths leading to it.

  • The chain path: she teaches danger signs, the mother recognises them, the mother gets to a clinic in time.
  • The shared-parent path: the same programme that put Beatrice in the village also paid for the new ambulance.
  • The distance path: closer clinics make every other arrow more powerful.

Volunteers look small. The system around them is what makes them powerful.

Try it yourself

You are the analyst

scenario

A new programme in a neighbouring district will train CHWs to use a smartphone app for danger-sign screening. The pitch deck claims: “smartphone screening will reduce neonatal mortality by 20%”.

Before you fund it: draw the picture. What boxes go on it? Which arrows have signs you can guess? Which arrows are you uncertain about?

Hint: where does “the smartphone” go on the picture? Where does “phone signal” go? Where does “literacy” go?

In Chapter 3, we will turn this exercise into a real method — building a causal picture by sitting down and interviewing the people who run the programme.

The one thing to remember

A causal diagram is just three node types, signed arrows, and three shapes.

That is the whole grammar. Once you know it, you can draw a picture for any programme you encounter — and the picture will tell you which questions are answerable.
