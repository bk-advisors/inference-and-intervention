**Protagonist:** David, 52, county budget director, Nakuru County, Kenya. Composite from county budget circulars and Auditor-General reports.

## Slide: Meet David

David is fifty-two. He is the county budget director for Nakuru County, Kenya. *(pause)*

Same disclosure as always. David is a composite drawn from publicly available county budget circulars and Auditor-General reports. The details are real, the name is not. *(pause)*

David has an envelope on his desk. Inside the envelope, there is enough money for *one* new midwife. One salary line for the next twelve months. *(pause)*

Two facilities are competing for her. *(pause)*

Facility one is in **Nakuru town**. Busy. On the highway. Already has three midwives. The town hospital is the kind of place where if you arrive bleeding, somebody will see you within fifteen minutes. *(pause)*

Facility two is in **Bahati ward**. Rural. On a rough road that turns to mud in the rainy season. Currently has *zero* midwives. Births there are done by traditional birth attendants — wonderful women, but no formal training, no oxytocin. *(pause)*

Both facility managers have written David passionate letters. Both letters are convincing. *(pause)*

Now look at the box on the screen. **Where does the midwife go — and how do you make that decision in a way you can defend at the next public budget hearing?** *(pause)*

That is the question of this chapter. And the answer is going to use everything we built in Part 2.

## Slide: And here is the puzzle

The honest answer is that David could just go with his gut. Many county budget officers do exactly that. They have been on the job for years. They know which facility is run by whom. They make the call. *(pause)*

And sometimes the gut call is right. *(pause)*

But David is at a point in his career where he wants more than that. He wants a *number*. He wants to be able to walk into the next public budget hearing — and these are real, public, often televised — and say: *"Placing her in Bahati saves an estimated X more newborn lives per year than placing her in Nakuru town, with this much uncertainty."* *(pause)*

That sentence is not a gut call. That sentence is something a journalist can fact-check. That sentence is something a county assembly member can argue with. That sentence is *defendable*. *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **To get that sentence, David needs three things.** The picture from Chapters 2 and 3. The lookup tables from Chapter 4. And one new tool, just for this chapter — a way to attach a *decision* to the picture. *(pause)*

That tool is called a decision map. Let me show you.

## Slide: A new shape on the picture

Until now, all the boxes on our pictures have been *things that happen* — facts about the world. The mother is poor. The baby survives. The facility has a stockout. *(pause)*

Today we add a new shape. A box for *things you choose*. *(pause)*

Look at the definition box. **Three shapes for decision pictures.** *(pause)*

An **oval** is an uncertain event — something the world decides for you. The mother's complications. The road conditions. We have been drawing those for six chapters. *(pause)*

A **rectangle** is a decision — something *you* choose. Where to place the midwife. Whether to fund the SMS programme. We are adding this shape today. *(pause)*

A **hexagon** is the outcome you care about — the thing you are ultimately trying to move. We have been drawing this from the beginning, in red. *(pause)*

Look at the box. **A picture with all three shapes is called a decision map.** That is the new tool. We are going to use it for the rest of the course.

## Slide: David's decision map

Here is David's decision map.

*Look at the diagram.* At the top: a *rectangle* — "place midwife in Bahati or Nakuru?" That is the decision. The thing David chooses. *(pause)*

Below the rectangle, three ovals. Volume of complicated births. Distance to the next-nearest facility. Quality of supervision. Three uncertain things David has *opinions* about but does not *know*. *(pause)*

And at the bottom, the hexagon. "Newborn lives saved this year." That is the outcome. *(pause)*

Look at the green box. **The rectangle at the top is David's choice. The ovals in the middle are things he is uncertain about. The hexagon is what he cares about.** *(pause)*

Notice the structure. The decision sits at the top. The uncertainties sit in the middle. The outcome sits at the bottom. Decisions flow into uncertainties flow into outcomes. That is the standard layout for a decision map. Use it whenever you draw one.

## Slide: Expected value, in counts (section divider)

*(pause for transition)*

OK. We have a picture. Now we need to put numbers on it and use those numbers to choose. The way we do that has a name: **expected value**. And we are going to do it with counts, the same way we did everything in Chapter 4.

---

## Slide: What "expected value" really means

Forget the textbook definition for a moment. Let me show you what it actually is.

Suppose Bahati has two possible futures over the next twelve months. *(pause)*

The good year: the rains come on time, the road stays passable, the new midwife integrates well, and **eight newborn lives are saved that would have died otherwise**. The probability of the good year is sixty out of one hundred. *(pause)*

The bad year: the rains fail, the road washes out, the midwife is alone for weeks at a time, and **only two lives are saved**. The probability is forty out of one hundred. *(pause)*

The two probabilities add to one hundred — see Chapter 5. *(pause)*

Now look at the definition box. **Expected value equals the average over the futures, weighted by how likely each future is.** *(pause)*

For Bahati: sixty futures out of a hundred save eight lives, and forty futures out of a hundred save two lives. So total lives saved across all hundred futures is sixty times eight plus forty times two — that is four hundred and eighty plus eighty, which is five hundred and sixty. Divide by one hundred futures, and you get **five point six lives saved per year, on average**. *(pause)*

Look at the green box. **Counts. Not formulas.** Sixty out of one hundred futures save eight, forty out of one hundred save two. The maths is the same as the lookup-table maths from Chapter 4. We are just applying it to *futures* instead of *patients*.

---

## Slide: The same calculation for Nakuru town

Now Nakuru town. *(pause)*

The good year: things go smoothly, the new midwife joins the existing team of three, and *five lives are saved* that would otherwise have died. Probability eighty out of one hundred. *(pause)*

The bad year: there is a measles outbreak, or a strike, or some other disruption, and only one life is saved. Probability twenty out of one hundred. *(pause)*

Notice — Nakuru's bad year is *more likely to be only mildly bad*, because the existing team can absorb shocks. The variance is smaller. *(pause)*

Expected value: eighty times five plus twenty times one — four hundred plus twenty, equals four hundred and twenty. Divide by one hundred. **Four point two lives saved per year on average.** *(pause)*

Look at the box. **Bahati: five point six. Nakuru: four point two.** On these numbers, the midwife should go to Bahati. *(pause)*

That is David's first answer. It is not a gut call. It is a *defensible number*. He could write it on a sticky note and put it in the envelope. He could read it out loud at the budget hearing. *(pause)*

But before he does, there is one more sentence in this chapter he needs to understand. The most important sentence in the whole chapter.

## Slide: Watching ≠ Doing (section divider)

*(pause for transition)*

---

## Slide: The most important sentence in the chapter

David has data on his desk. The data shows that facilities with more midwives have better newborn outcomes. He could just *stare at the data* and conclude: more midwives equals more lives saved. *(pause)*

But pause for a second. *(pause)* Where did that data come from? *(pause)*

Look at the bad-example box. **That data was observed.** It tells you about facilities that *already chose* to staff up. Those facilities might be wealthier. They might be in safer locations. They might have better roads, better leadership, better supply chains. *(pause)*

Now look at the good-example box. **David is not observing. He is acting.** He is picking up a midwife and *putting her somewhere*. The two questions are different. *(pause)*

When the data says *"facilities with more midwives have better outcomes"*, the data is telling you about the *world as it already arranged itself*. When David asks *"if I add a midwife to this specific facility, how much better will the outcome be?"*, he is asking about an *intervention* — a change he makes to the world. *(pause)*

These two questions can have very different answers. *(pause)*

Look at the definition box. **This is the difference between inference (watching) and intervention (doing).** *(pause)*

The whole title of this course is right here. *Inference and Intervention*. Six chapters on inference. Now four chapters on intervention. The shift is real.

---

## Slide: Graph surgery in plain language

There is a technical move that captures this shift. It has an unfortunate name. It is called **graph surgery**. Don't be put off by the name. *(pause)*

Here is what graph surgery actually is. When you intervene on a box on a picture — say, when David picks up a midwife and *puts her* in Bahati — you have to *cut all the arrows pointing into that box*. Because *you* are now the cause of that box, not whatever else used to be. *(pause)*

Look at the box. *On David's picture, the box "midwife placement" had arrows from things like facility caseload and community pressure. When David decides to place her, those arrows get cut. He is the one doing the placing, not the caseload.* *(pause)*

That is graph surgery. Cutting the incoming arrows of the box you are intervening on. *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **Numbers from observed data are not always the same as numbers from interventional data.** *(pause)*

A picture lets you *see* when they will diverge. The picture tells you which arrows get cut by an intervention, and which numbers therefore stop applying. That is the value of having drawn the picture in the first place.

## Slide: Value of information (section divider)

*(pause for transition)*

OK. One more idea. This one is the one most people in our field have never heard of, and it is one of the most useful in the whole course.

---

## Slide: When is more research worth the wait?

David's numbers were uncertain. His estimates of "good year" and "bad year" were rough guesses. *(pause)*

He could commission a study. A one-month study of Bahati's actual complication rate. He could send a team out with a clipboard and they could come back with much better numbers. *(pause)*

But there is a cost. The study delays the placement by a month. The midwife is not helping anyone in the meantime. *(pause)*

So the question is: **would the new study actually change his decision?** *(pause)*

Look at the orange box. **If the answer is no, the study is not worth doing.** *(pause)*

That sentence is more important than it looks. Most studies in global health are commissioned without anyone asking whether the result would actually change a decision. They are commissioned because *information feels like progress*. It is not. Information is only valuable if it changes something. Otherwise it is just expensive paper.

---

## Slide: EVPI in plain language

So how do you tell? There is a tool. It has an unfortunate acronym — **EVPI**, which stands for **Expected Value of Perfect Information**. The acronym is bad. The idea is good. *(pause)*

Look at the definition box. **Imagine a magical study that told you the answer with one hundred percent certainty. By how much would your average outcome improve?** *(pause)*

If the answer is "by a lot", more research is worth doing. *(pause)* If the answer is "by very little", more research is wasted — even a *perfect* study would not change your decision much, so an *imperfect* real study certainly will not. *(pause)*

In David's case, the perfect study would tell him for sure whether Bahati was going to have a good year or a bad year. With perfect information, he could make a better-informed call. The improvement in expected lives saved — across the futures where the perfect information would actually flip his decision — is the EVPI. *(pause)*

If David's EVPI is one tenth of a life per year, the study is not worth waiting for. If his EVPI is two lives per year, the study is essential. *(pause)*

Look at the green box. **This single idea — would more information actually change my decision? — quietly resolves more analyst-vs-manager arguments than any other tool in this course.** *(pause)*

When a manager says *"we need more research"*, you can ask: *"Would the answer change what we do? If not, why are we waiting?"*

---

## Slide: Three quiet warnings

Three warnings. Then we try it.

*Bullet by bullet.* **Warning one.** Expected value averages out the bad years. Sometimes you should not average. Sometimes the bad year is *catastrophic* — like running out of misoprostol — and you have to plan for it specifically. We will spend the whole of Chapter 8 on this. *(pause)*

**Warning two.** Your probabilities came from somewhere. Whose interview? Whose database? Whose hunch? Trust your numbers only as much as you trust their source. Tell the audience where each number came from. *(pause)*

**Warning three.** This is the most important one in Part 3. **A confident number from a wrong picture is the most dangerous thing in this chapter.** *(pause)* If your expected-value calculation looks crisp and impressive, but the picture underneath has a missing arrow, you are giving people a false sense of certainty. Always go back to Chapter 2 first. *Right picture beats precise number.*

## Slide: Try It (You are the analyst)

OK. Try it.

A donor offers your county a free newborn resuscitation device. Two hospitals want it. Hospital A is large and busy but has reliable electricity. Hospital B is smaller, rural, and has frequent power cuts. *(pause)*

You estimate Hospital A would save three newborns per year with the device, with high confidence. You estimate Hospital B would save six newborns per year *if the power held*, but only one if it didn't — and you think the power holds about half the time. *(pause)*

**Compute the expected value for each hospital. Where does the device go?** *(pause)*

Hint. For hospital B, weight six by zero point five and one by zero point five. *(pause)*

Hospital A: three, with high confidence. So expected value about three. *(pause)*

Hospital B: half the time six, half the time one. That is point five times six plus point five times one — three plus point five, equals three point five. *(pause)*

Hospital B wins by half a newborn a year. The device goes to the rural hospital. *(pause)*

But notice — that decision came down to the assumption *"the power holds about half the time."* If the power actually holds only thirty percent of the time, the expected value of B drops to two point five, and Hospital A wins. **This is exactly the kind of question where EVPI matters.** A one-week study of B's actual power reliability could change the decision. So commission it.
