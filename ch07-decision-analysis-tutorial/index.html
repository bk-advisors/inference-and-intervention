Meet David

David, 52, county budget director, Nakuru County, Kenya (a composite drawn from county budget circulars and Auditor-General reports)

David has an envelope on his desk with enough money for one new midwife.

Two facilities are competing for her. One in Nakuru town — busy, on the highway, already has three midwives. One in Bahati ward — rural, on a rough road, currently has none.

Both managers have written him passionate letters.

The question: Where does the midwife go — and how do you make that decision in a way you can defend at the next public budget hearing?

And here is the puzzle

David could just go with his gut. (Many do.)

But he wants a number. He wants to be able to say: “Placing her in Bahati saves an estimated X more newborn lives per year than placing her in Nakuru town, with this much uncertainty.”

To get that sentence, he needs three things: the picture from Chapters 2–3, the lookup tables from Chapter 4, and one new tool — a way to attach a decision to the picture.

Decision maps

A new shape on the picture

Until now, our boxes have all been things that happen — facts about the world. Today we add a new shape for things you choose.

Three shapes for decision pictures:

  • Oval = an uncertain event (the world decides)
  • Rectangle = a decision (you choose)
  • Hexagon = the outcome you care about (what you are trying to move)

A picture with all three is called a decision map.

Expected value, in counts

What “expected value” really means

Suppose Bahati has two possible futures over the next year:

  • The good year: 8 lives saved (probability: 60 out of 100)
  • The bad year: 2 lives saved (probability: 40 out of 100)

Expected value = the average over the futures, weighted by how likely each future is.

For Bahati: (60 × 8 + 40 × 2) ÷ 100 = (480 + 80) ÷ 100 = 5.6 lives saved per year, on average.

Counts. Not formulas. “60 out of 100 futures save 8, 40 out of 100 save 2.” The maths is the same as Chapter 4.

The same calculation for Nakuru town

  • The good year: 5 lives saved (probability: 80 out of 100)
  • The bad year: 1 life saved (probability: 20 out of 100)

Nakuru: (80 × 5 + 20 × 1) ÷ 100 = (400 + 20) ÷ 100 = 4.2 lives saved per year, on average.

Bahati: 5.6. Nakuru: 4.2. On these numbers, the midwife should go to Bahati.

Watching ≠ Doing

The most important sentence in the chapter

David has data showing facilities with more midwives have better newborn outcomes. He could just stare at the data and conclude: more midwives = more lives saved.

But that data was observed. It tells you about facilities that already chose to staff up. They might be wealthier, in safer locations, with better roads.

David is not observing. He is acting. He is picking up a midwife and putting her somewhere. The two questions are different.

This is the difference between inference (watching) and intervention (doing). The whole course title is here.

Graph surgery in plain language

When you intervene on a box, you have to cut all the arrows pointing into it, because you are now the cause of that box, not whatever else used to be.

On David’s picture, the box “midwife placement” had arrows from things like facility caseload and community pressure. When David decides to place her, those arrows get cut. He is the one doing the placing, not the caseload.

Numbers from observed data are not always the same as numbers from interventional data. A picture lets you see when they will diverge.

Value of information

When is more research worth the wait?

David’s numbers above are uncertain. He could commission a one-month study of Bahati’s actual complication rate — but that delays the placement by a month, and the midwife is not helping anyone in the meantime.

Question: would the new study change his decision? If the answer is no, the study is not worth doing.

EVPI in plain language

Expected Value of Perfect Information (EVPI): imagine a magical study that told you the answer with 100% certainty. By how much would your average outcome improve?

If EVPI is small, more research is wasted. If EVPI is large, more research is essential. Either way, you have an answer about whether to wait.

This single idea — “would more information actually change my decision?” — quietly resolves more analyst-vs-manager arguments than any other tool in this course.

Three quiet warnings

  1. Expected value averages out the bad years. Sometimes you should not average — you should plan for the bad year specifically. (Ch 8.)
  2. Your probabilities came from somewhere. Whose interview? Whose database? Trust them only as much as you trust the source.
  3. A confident number from a wrong picture is the most dangerous thing in this chapter. Always go back to Chapter 2 first.

Try it yourself

You are the analyst

scenario

A donor offers your county a free newborn resuscitation device. Two hospitals want it. Hospital A is large and busy but has reliable electricity. Hospital B is smaller, rural, and has frequent power cuts.

You estimate Hospital A would save 3 newborns per year with the device, with high confidence. You estimate Hospital B would save 6 newborns per year if the power held, but only 1 if it didn’t — and you think the power holds about half the time.

Compute the expected value for each hospital. Where does the device go?

Hint: for hospital B, weight 6 by 0.5 and 1 by 0.5.

Further reading

The data behind David’s story

  • Office of the Auditor-General, Kenya — county budget execution reports, publicly available at oagkenya.go.ke.
  • Kenya County Public Expenditure Reviews (World Bank, 2014–2020) — open-access analyses of county-level health budgeting.
  • Nakuru County Health at a Glance, Ministry of Health (annual) — facility-level staffing and outcome data.

If you want to go deeper

  • Ryall, M. D. & Bramson, A. L. (2014) — Inference and Intervention. Routledge. Chapters 8–9 cover decision maps, expected value, and intervention vs observation in business settings.
  • Howard, R. A. & Abbas, A. E. (2015) — Foundations of Decision Analysis. The textbook on decision trees and EVPI. Dense but careful.

The one thing to remember

A decision is just a question with consequences — and the value of more information is exactly how much it would change your decision.

David did not need a PhD. He needed a picture, two lookup tables, and the discipline to compute one expected value per option.
